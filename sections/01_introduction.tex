\section{Introduction}

Reliable cellular communication is vital during disasters and large-scale power outages, when affected populations and emergency responders depend on timely information exchange. In the modeled network-wide outage scenario, O-RAN Radio Units (RUs) operate using limited local battery energy. An O-RU performs radio-facing functions~\cite{oranScArchitecture}. Under these assumptions, an RU with an exhausted battery can become a critical point of failure for users who cannot be reassociated with another active RU.

Operators must balance the need for emergency connectivity with battery lifetime. For example, during the 2025 Iberian blackout, MEO reconfigured its mobile network and restricted mobile-data usage to preserve essential services~\cite{lusa2025meo}. Drawing on this approach to outage energy management, the present study evaluates three predefined baseline RU policies: Always Active (\policyabbr{AA}), where all non-depleted RUs remain active; Staggered Active (\policyabbr{SA}), where RU groups alternate between active and sleep states; and Threshold Staggered Active (\policyabbr{TSA}), where all RUs remain active until each RU reaches a configured battery threshold, after which staggered operation is initiated.

These policies exemplify the fundamental trade-off between energy consumption and service availability. Keeping RUs active maximizes radio availability in the modeled scenario but accelerates battery depletion. Conversely, placing selected RUs in a low-power sleep state conserves energy but may reduce the proportion of users served. While sleep-mode mechanisms are established for reducing cellular-network energy consumption~\cite{wu2015sleep}, outage management must also maintain an acceptable level of emergency connectivity. It remains uncertain whether simple predefined RU sleep schedules can preserve a specified emergency-service target while significantly extending battery lifetime during a network-wide outage.

Accordingly, this study addresses two research questions. First, how do predefined RU sleep policies affect emergency connectivity and RU battery lifetime in an O-RAN network during a power outage? Second, do the evaluated policies meet the configured emergency-service target, or do their limitations indicate a need for more adaptive outage-management policies?

In this study, the emergency-service target is defined as maintaining a served-user fraction of at least \(0.70\) throughout the outage. To address the research questions, this paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It formulates a simplified, scenario-specific O-RAN outage model that captures RU battery depletion, active and sleep states, stochastic link quality, capacity-constrained user association, and emergency-service measures.
    \item It conducts controlled, paired simulations of \policyabbr{AA}, \policyabbr{SA}, and \policyabbr{TSA}, evaluating emergency Quality of Service (QoS), RU battery-depletion time, and Service Level Agreement (SLA)-based network lifetime.
    \item It quantifies the energy--service trade-off and assesses whether each predefined policy can meet the configured emergency-service target while extending battery availability.
\end{itemize}

Within the stated model configuration, \policyabbr{SA} achieves the greatest battery-lifetime extension but frequently fails to meet the emergency-service target early in the outage. In contrast, \policyabbr{TSA} maintains service lifetime comparable to \policyabbr{AA} but provides only a modest battery benefit. Therefore, while the evaluated fixed policies serve as useful reference points, they do not constitute a sufficient general basis for emergency outage management. More adaptive policy designs warrant further investigation. These conclusions are specific to the simplified model and experimental configuration and do not establish suitability for all real-world deployments.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II presents background and related work. Section~III defines the system model and evaluated policies. Section~IV describes the simulation methodology and experimental configuration. Section~V discusses the simulation results, and Section~VI concludes the paper.
