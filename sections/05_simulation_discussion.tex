\section{Simulation Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:simulation-results-discussion}

This section compares three predefined RU policies: Always Active (\policyabbr{AA}), Staggered Active (\policyabbr{SA}), and Threshold Staggered Active (\policyabbr{TSA}), evaluated over ten simulation repetitions.  The comparison jointly considers emergency connectivity, battery depletion, and SLA-based network lifetime, as improving one outcome at the expense of others cannot be adequately assessed by a single metric.  Table~\ref{tab:simulation-results-summary} presents the aggregate results, and Figures~\ref{fig:served-user-fraction-over-time}--\ref{fig:network-lifetime-by-policy}
are referenced in the following discussion to illustrate the observed trade-offs.

\subsection{Emergency Connectivity Performance}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{served_user_fraction_over_time_by_policy.png}
    \caption{Mean served-user fraction across repetitions for \policyabbr{AA}, \policyabbr{SA}, and \policyabbr{TSA}.}
    \label{fig:served-user-fraction-over-time}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:served-user-fraction-over-time} presents the mean served-user fraction at each timestamp, with the interquartile range across the ten repetitions shaded for each policy. All policies begin with a mean served-user fraction of approximately 0.86; however, their subsequent trajectories diverge significantly. \policyabbr{AA} maintains the strongest service trajectory during the early outage period. \policyabbr{TSA} initially mirrors \policyabbr{AA}, while \policyabbr{SA} exhibits an immediate reduction in service following the start of its staggered schedule. The mean served-user fraction drops below the 0.7 SLA threshold at time 21 for both \policyabbr{AA} and \policyabbr{TSA}, but at time 1 for \policyabbr{SA}. These threshold crossings describe the aggregate trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:served-user-fraction-over-time}; they are not the SLA-based network lifetimes of individual repetitions. The latter are calculated from each run's first SLA violation and are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:simulation-results-summary} and Figure~\ref{fig:network-lifetime-by-policy}. The periodic sleep schedule therefore reduces service availability precisely when continuous emergency connectivity is most critical.

The subsequent fluctuations in the \policyabbr{SA} trajectory do not indicate network recovery. These variations occur when alternating RU groups change state, altering the set of RUs available to serve users. As the model excludes battery recharge, all three trajectories decline as stored energy is depleted.  By the end of the 100-step horizon, the mean served-user fractions for all policies approach zero.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{average_emergency_qos_final_value_by_policy.png}
    \caption{Horizon-average emergency QoS by policy.}
    \label{fig:average-emergency-qos-by-policy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:average-emergency-qos-by-policy} supports this time-series interpretation by displaying the distribution of horizon-average QoS values.  \policyabbr{AA} achieves the highest median QoS (0.281), followed closely by \policyabbr{TSA} (0.278), while \policyabbr{SA} records the lowest value (0.268). \policyabbr{TSA} therefore incurs only a minor QoS reduction relative to \policyabbr{AA}, whereas \policyabbr{SA} experiences a substantially greater reduction. \policyabbr{TSA} remains similar to \policyabbr{AA}, while the \policyabbr{SA} distribution is consistently lower.

\subsection{Battery Depletion and the Energy--Service Trade-off}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ru_battery_status_over_time_by_policy.png}
    \caption{RU battery status over time by policy.}
    \label{fig:ru-battery-status-over-time}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:ru-battery-status-over-time} illustrates the average remaining battery of each RU across the repetitions, demonstrating the anticipated energy-saving effect of placing RUs in sleep mode. Under \policyabbr{AA}, all feasible RUs remain active, resulting in the most rapid battery depletion.  \policyabbr{SA} preserves battery charge longer, as only one fixed RU group is selected during each segment of the staggered cycle. \policyabbr{TSA} represents an intermediate approach: it initially operates like \policyabbr{AA} and transitions to staggered operation only after a battery-based trigger is activated.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{average_ru_battery_depletion_final_value_by_policy.png}
    \caption{Mean RU battery-depletion time by policy.}
    \label{fig:average-ru-battery-depletion-by-policy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:average-ru-battery-depletion-by-policy} summarizes this pattern by presenting the mean depletion time across the five RUs in each repetition. The median depletion times are 31.2 simulation steps for \policyabbr{AA}, 46.2 for \policyabbr{SA}, and 33.4 for \policyabbr{TSA}.  Compared to \policyabbr{AA}, \policyabbr{SA} increases the median depletion time by about 48\%, while \policyabbr{TSA} increases it by about 7\%. Both sleep-based policies extend the mean RU depletion time in every repetition, indicating that their energy benefit is consistent and not attributable to a single atypical run.

These results illustrate the energy--service trade-off. \policyabbr{SA} provides the greatest extension of battery life, but this benefit comes with the weakest service trajectory and the lowest average QoS. \policyabbr{TSA} achieves a smaller yet consistent improvement in battery life while maintaining service levels much closer to those of \policyabbr{AA}. This outcome aligns with the policy logic: delaying sleep scheduling preserves early service availability but reduces the remaining outage horizon available for energy savings.

\subsection{SLA-Based Network Lifetime and Policy Suitability}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{network_lifetime_final_value_by_policy.png}
    \caption{SLA-based network lifetime by policy.}
    \label{fig:network-lifetime-by-policy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:network-lifetime-by-policy} evaluates the policies using SLA-based network lifetime, a stricter metric than horizon-average QoS. SLA-based network lifetime is defined as the final timestamp before the first instance in which the served-user fraction falls below the SLA threshold. A value of zero indicates that the SLA was not maintained beyond the onset of the observed outage, and it does not imply that the network provided no service during the simulation. Likewise, subsequent increases in the served-user fraction cannot restore SLA-based network lifetime once an earlier SLA violation has occurred.

\policyabbr{AA} achieves a median network lifetime of 21.0 steps, while \policyabbr{TSA} closely matches this with a median of 20.5 steps.  \policyabbr{TSA} equals \policyabbr{AA}'s network lifetime in eight out of ten repetitions and is lower in the remaining two. In contrast, \policyabbr{SA} exhibits a median network lifetime of zero and an interquartile range of 0.0--17.3 steps. These findings indicate that \policyabbr{SA}'s periodic staggering frequently fails to meet the SLA before it can deliver a sustained emergency service interval, despite its subsequent battery advantage.

Thus, SLA-based network lifetime alters the interpretation of the battery results. If the primary objective were to delay average RU depletion, \policyabbr{SA} would be the preferred policy. However, for an emergency network aiming to maintain the configured service target without interruption, \policyabbr{SA} is not appropriate. \policyabbr{TSA} is more suitable, as it preserves nearly all of \policyabbr{AA}'s SLA-based network lifetime while providing a systematic, though modest, extension of battery life.
