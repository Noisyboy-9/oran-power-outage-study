\section{Related Work}

This section establishes the present study within the broader context of research on energy-efficient radio access and network reliability. It first examines the impact of sleep modes on service availability, then reviews literature on outage resilience and backup energy to clarify the specific research gap addressed by this evaluation.

\subsection{Sleep Modes and Energy-Aware Radio Access Management}

Sleep-mode operation represents a well-established strategy for reducing energy consumption in cellular radio access. Surveys in~\cite{wu2015sleep,lopezPerez2022ranEnergy} form part of the current state of the art around determining when and which radio resources can transition to lower-power states without unacceptable degradation in network performance. These studies demonstrate that sleep management is typically driven by fluctuations in traffic demand and may be integrated with mechanisms such as cell switching, carrier deactivation, user reassociation, and, more recently, data-driven control. The extent of energy savings achieved depends on the capacity of the remaining active infrastructure to absorb traffic and maintain service quality.

Subsequent studies further clarify the trade-off between energy efficiency and service quality. Authors at~\cite{son2011energyDelay} examines joint base-station operation and user-association mechanisms, specifically addressing the balance between energy consumption and user delay. A key finding is that switching decisions cannot be evaluated independently of association decisions, as deactivating a base station affects both network energy usage and the service experienced by reassociated users. Similarly,~\cite{oh2013dynamicSwitching} analyzes dynamic base-station switching strategies for green cellular networks, demonstrating that switching policies must balance the energy savings from deactivating infrastructure with the coverage and capacity provided by the remaining active stations.

These studies complement the present work, as their primary focus is energy-efficient operation in green cellular networks, often using optimization-based control. Such methods can achieve better energy-service trade-offs by adapting to real-time traffic and network conditions. In contrast, this study investigates a network-wide grid-power outage scenario where O-RUs rely on finite local batteries and the main objective is to maintain emergency connectivity. The evaluation considers whether three predefined policies (Always Active, Staggered Active, and Threshold Staggered Active) are adequate as emergency-outage baselines. While these policies are designed for understandability, they do not adapt to instantaneous traffic and link quality. Thus, the study does not aim to surpass adaptive sleep-management approaches but to establish a controlled reference point for future comparative evaluations.

\subsection{Cellular Resilience During Power Outages}

Research on cellular resilience investigates how communication networks can maintain availability during disasters, infrastructure failures, and power outages. Authors at~\cite{gomez2014disasterResilient,zhang2016selfOrganization,karaman2025resilientInfrastructure} examine resilience at the broader network level, considering distributed architectures, self-organization, backup-energy provision, and recovery mechanisms. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that emergency connectivity relies not only on radio coverage but also on power availability and the network's capacity to reconfigure when standard infrastructure is compromised.

Backup-energy management becomes especially critical when grid power is unavailable. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2019backupBattery} analyzes and allocates backup batteries for cellular base stations, demonstrating that both battery capacity and its distribution across the network significantly influence service interruptions during outages. This work focuses on the provisioning problem, specifically how backup energy should be allocated prior to an outage. In contrast, the present study addresses a similar operational issue: after the outage starts, how do predefined RU active--sleep policies affect emergency service and battery lifetime? These perspectives are complementary; battery allocation determines the total energy available, while sleep management governs how that energy is utilized during the outage.

Advanced simulators, emulators, and O-RAN experimentation platforms, including~\cite{patriciello2019e2e,nardini2020simu5g,bonati2021colosseum,bonatiOpenRANGym}, can model or validate radio-network behaviour with high physical- and protocol-level fidelity. While these tools offer increased realism, their configuration and interpretation can become complex, particularly when the research objective is to isolate the effects of a limited set of policies. The custom simulator employed in this study deliberately abstracts from deployment-specific power architectures, handover costs, and detailed physical-layer modelling. Although this approach reduces realism, it facilitates controlled comparisons of predefined policies under consistent placements, link variations, and battery assumptions.

In summary, prior research demonstrates the importance of energy-aware radio-access management, dynamic switching, backup-energy provision, and resilient network reconfiguration. The present study addresses the gap of comparatively evaluating simple predefined RU sleep policies within a unified network-wide outage model that incorporates finite RU batteries and a defined emergency-service objective. The primary contribution is empirical evidence on the limitations and trade-offs of these baseline policies, rather than the introduction of a new optimal or adaptive outage-management controller.
